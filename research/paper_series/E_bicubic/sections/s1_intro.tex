\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{The problem}

A \emph{planar map} is a connected graph drawn on the sphere without crossings, loops and multiple edges allowed,
considered up to deformation. It is \emph{cubic} if every vertex has degree three, and \emph{bicubic} if moreover its
vertices can be colored black and white so that every edge joins a black and a white vertex. A \emph{Hamiltonian
cycle} is a cycle through every vertex exactly once. We count triples (bicubic planar map with $2n$ vertices,
Hamiltonian cycle, one edge of the cycle marked and oriented); the marked edge removes all symmetry. Their number is
$z_n$:
\[ z_1,z_2,\ldots \;=\; 2,\ 8,\ 40,\ 228,\ 1424,\ 9520,\ 67064,\ 492292,\ \ldots \]
This is sequence A116456 of the OEIS~\cite{OEISbicubic}. Concatenating the codes of two configurations
(Section~\ref{sec:words}) gives $z_{m+n}\ge z_mz_n$, so the limit
\[ \mu^2 \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} z_n^{1/n} \;=\; \sup_n z_n^{1/n} \]
exists by Fekete's lemma~\cite{Fekete}. We write the growth rate as a square, as the physics literature does,
because $z_n$ counts maps with $2n$ vertices: $\mu$ is the growth per vertex. (In this paper $\mu$ is always this
bicubic growth rate, not the meander growth rate of Papers~A--D.)

The problem comes from physics. Guitter, Kristjansen and Nielsen~\cite{GKN} introduced it as a model of
two-dimensional quantum gravity coupled to matter of central charge $c=-1$, and argued that
$z_n\approx\mathrm{const}\cdot\mu^{2n}n^{\gamma-2}$ with the irrational exponent $\gamma=-(1+\sqrt{13})/6$. The same
model is the bicubic counterpart of meanders: both count pairs of non-crossing arch systems on the two sides of a line,
and in both the physical prediction rests on a coupling of matter and gravity that has not been proved
(for meanders see~\cite{PaperA}). Di~Francesco,
Duplantier, Golinelli and Guitter~\cite{DDGG} computed $z_n$ for $n\le 28$ and estimated
\[ \mu^2 \;=\; 10.113\pm0.001 \]
(their equation~(60)). This is a numerical estimate, not a bound; the authors write that the predictions remain
conjectural.

\subsection{Previous bounds and the main result}

The only rigorous bounds we found are those of Guitter, Kristjansen and Nielsen~\cite{GKN}, Section~5. In arch
language a configuration is two systems of non-crossing arches on $2n$ points of a line, one above and one below,
every arch joining an even and an odd point. Forgetting the parity condition gives at most $c_nc_{n+1}$
configurations, with $c_n$ the Catalan numbers, hence $\mu^2\le16$. Conversely, a single arch system above the line
automatically satisfies the parity condition, and flipping any subset of its $n$ arches below the line gives $2^nc_n$
distinct configurations, hence $\mu^2\ge 8$. Later papers~\cite{DDGG},~\cite{DGGbicolored} study exponents
numerically and state no bounds.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every $n\ge1$,
\[ z_n\;\le\;\Bigl(\frac{3187011595}{10^9}\Bigr)^{2n},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
   \mu^2\le 10.157042906664444025\ldots<10.15705 ; \]
and
\[ \mu^2\;\ge\;10.086918840819 . \]
\end{theorem}

So $\mu^2$ is known to within $0.071$, and the estimate $10.113$ lies $0.026$ above the lower bound and $0.044$
below the upper bound.

\subsection{The methods in outline}

Both bounds work on a machine with two stacks (Section~\ref{sec:words}). Read the vertices along the Hamiltonian cycle
one at a time. Each vertex has one edge that is not on the cycle, a \emph{chord}, drawn inside or outside the cycle.
The open chords on each side form a stack, and since the map is bipartite, all that matters about an open chord is the
parity of the position where it was opened. The configurations of size $n$ are exactly the closed walks of $2n$ moves
from the empty state.

The upper bound (Section~\ref{sec:upper}) is the method of Paper~B~\cite{PaperB}, Sections~3 and~4, in its simplest
form:
a positive weight on states that no step increases by more than a factor $R$ in total gives $z_n\le R^{2n}$. The
weight looks at the top twelve symbols of each stack and is stored as a table of $67$ million integers.

The lower bound (Section~\ref{sec:lower}) is the entropy method of Paper~B, Sections~5 and~6. One constructs a random rule
for the next move that looks only at the top symbols of the stacks and that is stationary: the stacks seen from the
top look statistically the same at every time. If the rule makes on average $e^h$ equally likely choices per move,
there are about $e^{ht}$ closed walks of $t$ moves. For meanders, making the rule exactly stationary was the main
difficulty and needed a large exact linear solve and many corrections. Here it is free. The two-stack machine is
reversible: every move can be undone by a move. Intuitively, a rule that is in balance across every move, as much flow
forward as backward, is automatically stationary, and such rules are given by arbitrary positive numbers on the
states. The work that remains is to choose good numbers and to bound the entropy.

\subsection{Status of the results}

The encoding by two-stack words and the upper certificate were obtained earlier in this project; the encoding was
checked in a separate independent reading, which also recounted $z_1,\ldots,z_{10}$ from the definition and counted
the maps for $n\le4$ without the word model, and which confirmed an upper certificate of radius~$7$ on all states with
at most $22$ open chords. The radius-$12$ certificate of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} was first checked by two programs written
by one author; for this paper it was checked a third time by a program written separately from the mathematical
statement alone (Section~\ref{sec:checks}). The lower bound is new. Its theorem (Theorem~L) adapts the entropy
theorem of Paper~B; the proof of the adaptation, the reversible construction (Proposition~\ref{prop:rev}) and the
closing lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:sweep}) were checked in a separate independent reading. The lower certificate was
checked by two separately written programs, one of which checks every stationarity equation directly without using
Proposition~\ref{prop:rev}, and a third time by a program written in that independent reading. Nothing has been reviewed outside the project, and no proof
assistant was used. Programs, proofs and certificates are in the project repository~\cite{Repo};
Appendix~\ref{app:where} lists where each proof is.
